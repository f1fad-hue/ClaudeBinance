Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

SGOV
Tailwind
A tightening-biased Fed is the one regime where a 0–3 month bill sleeve is paid to wait. Yielding 3.66% against 3.4% headline CPI, it roughly holds real value with near-zero duration — and every hawkish surprise that hurts the other three sleeves raises its reinvestment rate.
QQQ
Two-sided
The strongest single beneficiary of the capex and productivity boom the Fed is describing — and the sleeve most exposed to the 10-year at 4.78%. Long-duration equity discounts back at a rising rate. Earnings support is real; the multiple is the risk.
IEMG
Tailwind
Captures the fastest-growing bloc — China near 5%, India near 6.4% — at a valuation discount to US large-cap growth. A Fed on hold rather than hiking caps dollar strength, historically the single biggest swing factor for EM equity returns.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening policy drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and its correlation to QQQ means it fails precisely when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification.
The core tension. Growth is genuine and broad, but it is being met by tightening policy, a rising long end and record-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full 10-year horizon while shifting the equity mix toward the cheaper, faster-growing bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than to avoid it.

Term structure math

Volatility scales with the square root of time: σh = σann × √(h/12). From VIX 14.32 the implied S&P moves are:

Where this understates risk. Square-root scaling assumes constant volatility and normal returns. Both fail in stress: volatility clusters, and equity tails are fatter than lognormal. At VIX 14 the market is pricing a 12-month band of ±23.6% while the 10-year average VIX sits near 19 — implied volatility is roughly 25–30% cheap to its own history heading into a September FOMC where three members already want to hike.

Ten-year regime view

Over a decade a portfolio of this composition should expect to live through two to three volatility regime shifts. QQQ's own history sets the honest bound on what the equity sleeve can do:

2000–02 dot-com−83.0%
2007–09 financial crisis−53.5%
2022 rate-hiking cycle−37.0%

The 2022 episode is the closest analogue to the present configuration — a rate-driven de-rating rather than a credit event — and is what the −40% QQQ drawdown assumption is anchored to. The dot-com figure is the reminder that concentration risk in this index has no theoretical floor, and the reason the equity core is split across two blocs rather than held in one.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Ten years is long enough that cash drag compounds into a serious cost — SGOV's $13,570 terminal value against QQQ's $22,197 on the same $10,000 is the price of caution. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not on how much equity to own. Total risk assets stay at 75% in both portfolios.
The equity rotation follows the rankings. Asia/EM outscores the US at every horizon — 6.5 vs 5.0 at three months, 7.5 vs 7.0 at ten years. Moving 10 points from QQQ to IEMG brings the equity core to an even 35/35 split. It stops short of a full overweight because the US advantage compounds as the horizon extends, and the 10-year gap is only half a point.
IEMG is the rare sleeve that improves both sides. Forecast 8.60% net against QQQ's 8.30%, on lower volatility (18% vs 21%), a shallower drawdown (−33% vs −40%) and half the fee. Adding it improves return and reduces risk simultaneously — which is why the optimization requires no trade-off.
SGOV stays at 25% as ammunition, not as fear. With a composite reading of 4.8/10, cheap volatility and a 10-year at post-2023 highs, a repricing is a live risk. Holding it in 0–3 month bills rather than long bonds means the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields, and it earns 3.66% while waiting. Its function is to be sold into the drawdown to rebalance back to target — a 25% reserve is what converts a −27% drawdown from a loss into a buying opportunity.
BMNR is capped at 5% because of risk, not weight. At a twentieth of the capital it contributes 23.6% of portfolio variance — a 4.7× risk-to-weight ratio. It is also a poor diversifier: 0.65 correlated to QQQ, it fails in the same regimes as the growth sleeve. Held at 1.10× NAV with heavy dilution history and an 80.5% drawdown already on the tape, 5% is the largest defensible position. It earns its place as convexity to an ETH recovery, sized so that a total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.117% purely as a by-product of the rotation, since IEMG charges 0.09% against QQQ's 0.18%. Over ten years that is $16 saved per $10,000 — small, but it is return taken with no risk added, and QQQ's own cut from 0.20% is worth roughly $25 per $10,000 over the same period.
Recommendation. Adopt the optimized allocation — QQQ 35 / IEMG 35 / SGOV 25 / BMNR 5 — and rebalance to target semi-annually or whenever any sleeve drifts more than 5 points. Rebalancing is doing most of the work here: it mechanically trims the sleeve that has run and buys the one that has fallen, which is what turns BMNR's 95% volatility from a liability into a small, systematic source of return. Revisit the regional tilt if the Fed hikes rather than holds, if the 10-year breaks decisively above 5%, or if the US–Asia ranking gap closes below half a point.
