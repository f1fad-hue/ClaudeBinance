Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

SGOV

A hiking Fed is the one regime where a 0–3 month bill sleeve is paid to wait, and at 90% odds that hike is close to locked in. Its 3.63% SEC yield against 3.4% headline CPI leaves a real return near +0.2% at near-zero duration — thin, and the September CPI carrying the Saudi shock could take it negative.
QQQ
Two-sided
The strongest beneficiary of the capex and productivity boom the Fed describes — and the sleeve most exposed to rates, with the 10-year at 4.80% and the 2-year at ~4.36%, its highest since January 2025. Earnings support is real; the multiple is the risk.
IEMG
Tailwind
Captures the fastest-growing bloc — China 4.4% and India 6.3% for 2026, together 43.6% of global growth — at 11.7× forward against the Nasdaq-100’s 22.4×. But two headwinds now stack. Hike odds at 90% mean renewed dollar strength, and Brent at $104.61 is a terms-of-trade hit to a bloc that imports most of its energy. The overweight rests on a ten-year valuation gap, not the next FOMC.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and a 0.65 correlation to QQQ means it fails when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification — though the company has pivoted from accumulating ETH to buying back stock.
The core tension. Growth is genuine, but it is met by tightening policy, a rising long end, a realised energy shock and still-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full ten-year horizon while tilting the equity mix toward the cheaper bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than avoid it.

Two findings that constrained the answer

The cash line is straight
ret/DD 0.1322
Hold the risky mix in fixed proportion and scale it against cash, and return-per-drawdown is 0.1322 at every point — invariant to machine precision, because an uncorrelated zero-variance sleeve scales return and risk by the same factor. That is a theorem: no volatility analysis can identify a “correct” cash weight, only what risk each one carries. 30% here is a drawdown budget, labelled as the preference it is. Correction: earlier revisions claimed the illustration below was itself invariant. Pinning IEMG changes the risky mix, not just its scale, so it drifts from 0.137 to 0.126.
Variance math breaks on BMNR
69.6%
Under a lognormal model, a +10% compound return at 109% volatility implies an arithmetic mean of 69.6%. Nobody forecasts that, so the two assumptions are jointly incoherent and every variance-based statistic involving BMNR is approximate. A rebalancing-premium adjustment was computed, found to be dominated by this artefact, and discarded. It is a further argument for the 5% cap.

Over a decade a portfolio of this composition should expect to live through two to three volatility regime shifts. QQQ's own history sets the honest bound on what the equity sleeve can do:

The 2022 episode is the closest analogue to today — a rate-driven de-rating, not a credit event — and the −40% QQQ assumption sits deliberately beyond its worst reading. The dot-com figure is why the equity core is split across two blocs rather than held in one.

Is this the best available?

Every allocation the brief permits — all four sleeves, multiples of 5, none below 5% — is 969 portfolios. Plotting each by net CAGR against normalised drawdown gives the efficient frontier: the set where no other allocation offers both more return and less risk.

Both books now sit on the frontier. Searched exhaustively, nothing beats either on both axes at once — so both are efficient, and they differ only in where they sit on the curve. That is a change: last pass the baseline was dominated by three allocations. Correcting QQQ’s stale multiple lifted it onto the frontier. The optimized book is therefore no longer an improvement on the baseline; it is a lower-drawdown point on the same curve, bought with 0.43 points of CAGR.
Three honest limits. “Best” is a curve, not a point — which allocation is right depends on the drawdown you will actually tolerate, and both books are defensible answers — 90 allocations are efficient. The curve flattens here: at the recommendation, one more point of drawdown buys only about 0.16 points of CAGR. And beyond BMNR 45% the frontier returns drawdowns worse than −100%, which is impossible — the variance model fails exactly where this page already documents it failing, so the curve is drawn only where BMNR stays at 5%.

Bands are annualised percent, on the ten-year realised CAGR. The standard error is the book’s own volatility divided by √10 — the sampling error of a ten-year average, before any error in the inputs themselves.

No allocation on this page is provable inside its own horizon. Of the 969 admissible allocations, the number statistically distinguishable from the recommendation at 95% over ten years is 0. The reason is arithmetic, not modesty: for two books drawn from the same four sleeves, z ≈ IR × √T, and an information ratio of 0.26 gives z = 0.83 where 1.96 is needed. Establishing that the baseline really out-returns the recommendation would take 56 years; establishing that the recommended book beats plain T-bills would take 46. The most separable alternative in the whole set — QQQ 50 / IEMG 40 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — would still need 31.

Why the ranking is firmer than the number

The level is close to unknowable
±4.81 pts
One standard error on the ten-year outcome is 4.81 points — roughly two thirds of the forecast itself. Anyone quoting 7.56% to two decimals, this page included, is reporting the centre of a distribution running from about −2% to +17% at 95%. The six-figure terminal values elsewhere on this page carry the same error; they are shown to the dollar because the arithmetic is exact, not because the outcome is.
The comparison is nine times sharper
±0.52 pts
The two books share three of four sleeves, so their difference is a 10-point switch out of QQQ with a tracking volatility of just 1.65%/yr — a standard error of 0.52 points, not 4.81. Errors common to both books cancel. The 0.43-point gap is therefore 0.83 SE, which makes it an 80% chance the baseline ends ahead on return — worth acting on, even though it is nowhere near provable.
A correction to this page’s own arithmetic. An earlier pass put that figure at 53%, by dividing the 0.43-point gap by the ±4.81-point error on the level. Wrong denominator: a comparison of overlapping books is governed by the tracking error of the difference, not by either book’s absolute error. Against the right one the answer is 80%. The page had understated its own confidence in the ranking while overstating what it knew about the number.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Over ten years cash drag compounds into a real cost — SGOV’s $13,649 terminal value against QQQ’s $26,389 on the same $10,000. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not how much equity to own.
All three signals have now turned. The rankings still favour Asia and the 11.7×-against-22.4× gap still favours EM — but the return model has reversed twice in two passes. Correcting the terminal-multiple asymmetry put QQQ ahead on raw return; correcting its stale starting multiple has now put it ahead on risk-adjusted return too, 0.34 against 0.30. The EM position has been trimmed 40% → 35% → 30% and is now only five points above the baseline’s 25%.
The tilt is a risk trade, not a free lunch. IEMG forecasts 8.59% net against QQQ’s 10.19% — it earns less — and per unit of volatility it now trails too, 0.30 against 0.34. It buys a barely shallower drawdown (−39% against −40%) at half the fee, and nothing else. The claims that it improved both sides at once, and then that it kept a risk-adjusted edge, are both withdrawn.
Volatility sizes the book, it does not steer it. Re-running with volatility mean-reverted to 18.9 and correlations stressed to 0.85 leaves the Sharpe ordering of every candidate intact — so the tilt is robust to the regime. The risk estimate is not: −25.9% at today’s levels becomes −32.8% normalised. Sizing to the second is why the reserve is 30%.
Where the model was overruled. Subject only to the brief’s 5%-per-sleeve floor, the best return-per-unit-risk mix is QQQ 60 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — Sharpe 0.306 against the recommended 0.289. Rejected: it over-fits the two least reliable inputs and holds only 5% in reserve, which is not a drawdown budget anyone would choose. A rebalancing premium of an apparent +2.9%/yr was also computed and discarded as an artefact of BMNR’s 109% volatility.
SGOV at 30% is a preference, not an output. Scaling a fixed risky mix against cash holds return-per-drawdown at 0.1322 whatever the cash weight — the optimizer genuinely cannot pick it. 30% is a stated drawdown budget of roughly −33% in a normalised world. Held in 0–3 month bills, the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields and earns 3.63% while waiting.
BMNR is capped at 5% for risk, not weight. At a twentieth of capital it contributes 25.2% of variance — a 5.0× risk-to-weight ratio — and at 0.65 correlation to QQQ it fails in the same regimes. Verification cut its forecast this pass (2.61% staking, not 3.00%) while improving its balance sheet (0.98× total NAV, $4B of buybacks replacing dilution), but 5% remains the largest defensible size: total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.117% as a by-product of the rotation — $26 per $10,000 over ten years, taken with no risk added.
Recommendation. Adopt QQQ 35 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 if you want the lower drawdown — −32.8% normalised against the baseline’s −35.2%, bought with 0.43 points of CAGR. If you do not, the baseline is now the better book on every other measure, and this page says so rather than defending a tilt the evidence has stopped supporting. Rebalance semi-annually or on a 5-point drift. Revisit if the EM forward-multiple discount narrows from 40% toward its 25% norm — that is the thesis, and its closing removes the overweight entirely. If the VIX sustains a move above 18.9, the volatility premise has played out and 30% cash becomes a candidate to spend toward 25%.

The stale multiple, resolved. QQQ’s 25.2× forward P/E was flagged as the weakest input for three passes and is now corrected to 22.4×. Three independent routes agree: two secondary sources put the Nasdaq-100 at 20.8–22.4× in late August, and the trailing multiple fell 19% over the same window (35.24× → 28.55×), which drags the forward figure to about 20.4× on its own. The correction adds 1.16 points to QQQ and, with it, reverses the last remaining argument for the emerging-markets tilt: QQQ now leads on risk-adjusted return as well as raw, and the baseline has moved onto the efficient frontier.
