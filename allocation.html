Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

SGOV
Tailwind
A hiking Fed is the one regime where a 0–3 month bill sleeve is paid to wait, and at 90% odds that hike is close to locked in. Its 3.63% SEC yield against 3.4% headline CPI leaves a real return near +0.2% at near-zero duration — thin, and the September CPI carrying the Saudi shock could take it negative.
QQQ
Two-sided
The strongest beneficiary of the capex and productivity boom the Fed describes — and the sleeve most exposed to rates, with the 10-year at 4.80% and the 2-year at ~4.36%, its highest since January 2025. Earnings support is real; the multiple is the risk.
IEMG
Tailwind
Captures the fastest-growing bloc — China 4.4% and India 6.3% for 2026, together 43.6% of global growth — at 11.7× forward against the Nasdaq-100’s 25.2×. But two headwinds now stack. Hike odds at 90% mean renewed dollar strength, and Brent at $100 is a terms-of-trade hit to a bloc that imports most of its energy. The overweight rests on a ten-year valuation gap, not the next FOMC.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and a 0.65 correlation to QQQ means it fails when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification — though the company has pivoted from accumulating ETH to buying back stock.
The core tension. Growth is genuine, but it is met by tightening policy, a rising long end, a realised energy shock and still-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full ten-year horizon while tilting the equity mix toward the cheaper bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than avoid it.

Term structure math

Volatility scales with the square root of time: σh = σann × √(h/12). From VIX 15.30 the implied S&P moves are:

Where this understates risk. Square-root scaling assumes constant volatility and normal returns. Both fail in stress: volatility clusters and equity tails are fatter than lognormal. At VIX 15.30 the market is pricing a 12-month band of ±25.2% against a 2016–2023 average of 18.9 — implied volatility is still roughly 19% cheap to its own history, heading into a 15–16 September FOMC now 90% priced for a hike, an August CPI that came in hot on core, and an active conflict around the Strait of Hormuz.

Two findings that constrained the answer

The cash line is straight
ret/DD 0.1208
Hold the risky mix in fixed proportion and scale it against cash, and return-per-drawdown is 0.1208 at every point — invariant to machine precision, because an uncorrelated zero-variance sleeve scales return and risk by the same factor. That is a theorem: no volatility analysis can identify a “correct” cash weight, only what risk each one carries. 30% here is a drawdown budget, labelled as the preference it is. Correction: earlier revisions claimed the illustration below was itself invariant. Pinning IEMG changes the risky mix, not just its scale, so it drifts from 0.123 to 0.118.
Variance math breaks on BMNR
69.6%
Under a lognormal model, a +10% compound return at 109% volatility implies an arithmetic mean of 69.6%. Nobody forecasts that, so the two assumptions are jointly incoherent and every variance-based statistic involving BMNR is approximate. A rebalancing-premium adjustment was computed, found to be dominated by this artefact, and discarded. It is a further argument for the 5% cap.

Ten-year regime view

Over a decade a portfolio of this composition should expect to live through two to three volatility regime shifts. QQQ's own history sets the honest bound on what the equity sleeve can do:

2000–02 dot-com≈−83%
2007–09 financial crisis−50 to −53%
2020 COVID crash≈−27%
2022 rate-hiking cycle−33 to −37%

Figures vary by a few points on price versus total-return basis, so these are ranges. The 2022 episode is the closest analogue to today — a rate-driven de-rating, not a credit event — and the −40% QQQ assumption sits deliberately beyond its worst reading. The dot-com figure is why the equity core is split across two blocs rather than held in one.

Is this the best available?

Every allocation the brief permits — all four sleeves, multiples of 5, none below 5% — is 969 portfolios. Plotting each by net CAGR against normalised drawdown gives the efficient frontier: the set where no other allocation offers both more return and less risk.

The optimized book sits on the frontier; the baseline does not. Searched exhaustively, nothing beats QQQ 30 / IEMG 35 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 on both axes at once — so within this mandate it is efficient, not merely reasonable. The baseline is dominated by 3 allocations: QQQ 15 / IEMG 60 / SGOV 20 / BMNR 5 earns 0.14 points more at slightly less drawdown. That is the concrete sense in which the rotation is worth making.
Two honest limits. “Best” is a curve, not a point — 103 allocations are efficient, and which one is right depends on the drawdown you will actually tolerate. And the curve flattens here: at the recommendation, accepting one more point of drawdown buys only 0.06 points of CAGR, against 0.16 further down the curve. Pushing for more return is poor value from this position. Beyond BMNR 45% the frontier returns drawdowns worse than −100%, which is impossible — the variance model fails exactly where this page already documents it failing, so the curve is only drawn where BMNR stays at 5%.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Over ten years cash drag compounds into a real cost — SGOV’s $13,649 terminal value against QQQ’s $23,739 on the same $10,000. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not how much equity to own.
The third signal has reversed. The rankings and the 11.7×-vs-25.2× valuation gap still favour EM. The return model no longer does: correcting the terminal-multiple asymmetry puts QQQ 0.44 points ahead, where earlier revisions had IEMG ahead by 0.76. The tilt was trimmed from 40% to 35% accordingly.
The tilt is a risk trade, not a free lunch. IEMG forecasts 8.59% net against QQQ’s 9.03% — it earns less — but on 18% volatility against 21%, −33% drawdown against −40%, half the fee. Per unit of volatility it still leads, 0.30 against 0.28. The claim that it improved both sides at once is withdrawn.
Volatility sizes the book, it does not steer it. Re-running with volatility mean-reverted to 18.9 and correlations stressed to 0.85 leaves the Sharpe ordering of every candidate intact — so the tilt is robust to the regime. The risk estimate is not: −25.8% at today’s levels becomes −33.3% normalised. Sizing to the second is why the reserve is 30%.
Where the model was overruled. Subject only to the brief’s 5%-per-sleeve floor, the best return-per-unit-risk mix is QQQ 25 / IEMG 65 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — Sharpe 0.278 against the recommended 0.263. Rejected: it over-fits the two least reliable inputs and puts 65% in a bloc where China alone is a quarter of the index, on 5% reserve. A rebalancing premium of an apparent +2.9%/yr was also computed and discarded as an artefact of BMNR’s 109% volatility.
SGOV at 30% is a preference, not an output. Scaling a fixed risky mix against cash holds return-per-drawdown at 0.1208 whatever the cash weight — the optimizer genuinely cannot pick it. 30% is a stated drawdown budget of roughly −33% in a normalised world. Held in 0–3 month bills, the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields and earns 3.63% while waiting.
BMNR is capped at 5% for risk, not weight. At a twentieth of capital it contributes 24.7% of variance — a 4.9× risk-to-weight ratio — and at 0.65 correlation to QQQ it fails in the same regimes. Verification improved its standing (1.02× on total NAV, a $4B buyback replacing dilution), but 5% remains the largest defensible size: total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.112% as a by-product of the rotation — $32 per $10,000 over ten years, taken with no risk added.
Recommendation. Adopt the optimized allocation — QQQ 30 / IEMG 35 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 — rebalancing semi-annually or whenever a sleeve drifts more than 5 points. Rebalancing does much of the work: it trims what has run and buys what has fallen, which is what turns BMNR’s 95% volatility into a small systematic source of return. Revisit if the 10-year breaks decisively above 5%, or the EM forward-multiple discount narrows from 40% toward its 25% norm — that last is the thesis, and its closing is the signal to remove the overweight. If the VIX sustains a move above 18.9, the volatility premise has played out and 30% cash becomes a candidate to spend toward 25%.
