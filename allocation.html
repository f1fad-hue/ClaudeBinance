Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

SGOV
Tailwind
A hiking Fed is the one regime where a 0–3 month bill sleeve is paid to wait, and the hike has now landed: the range is 3.75–4.00% with more priced. Its pre-hike 3.74% SEC yield against 3.4% headline CPI left a real return near +0.3% at near-zero duration. The hike lifts that as bills roll over, but it stays thin, and the September CPI carrying the Saudi shock could still take it negative.
QQQ
Two-sided
The strongest beneficiary of the capex and productivity boom the Fed describes — and the sleeve most exposed to rates, with the 10-year at 5.12% on 23 September, its highest since 2007, a day after closing at 4.96%. Earnings support is real; the multiple is the risk.
IEMG
Tailwind
Captures the fastest-growing bloc — China 4.4% and India 6.3% for 2026, together 43.6% of global growth — at 11.5× forward against the Nasdaq-100’s 23.4×. But two headwinds now stack. A committee unanimous on tightening means renewed dollar strength, and Brent at $103.08 is a terms-of-trade hit to a bloc that imports most of its energy. The overweight rests on a ten-year valuation gap, not the next FOMC.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and a 0.65 correlation to QQQ means it fails when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification — though the company has pivoted from accumulating ETH to buying back stock.
The core tension. Growth is genuine, but it is met by tightening policy, a rising long end, a realised energy shock and still-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full ten-year horizon while tilting the equity mix toward the cheaper bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than avoid it.

Where this understates risk. Square-root scaling assumes constant volatility and normal returns. Both fail in stress: volatility clusters and equity tails are fatter than lognormal. At VIX 15.35 the market is pricing a 12-month band of ±25.3% against a 2016–2023 average of 18.9 — implied volatility is 18.8% cheap to its own history. It is pricing that calm against a Fed that has just hiked unanimously with 16 of 18 participants expecting at least one more, an August CPI whose monthly core overshot on a 5.4% producer-price pipeline, a 10-year at 5.12%, its highest since 2007, and an active conflict around the Strait of Hormuz.

Two findings that constrained the answer

The cash line is straight
ret/DD 0.1117
Hold the risky mix in fixed proportion and scale it against cash, and return-per-drawdown is 0.1117 at every point. With riskless cash that is exact — excess return and risk both scale by the risky weight, so the ratio cannot move. SGOV is not quite riskless: its 0.5% volatility leaves a residual of 7 × 10−6 along the line, invisible at any displayed precision. That is a theorem: no volatility analysis can identify a “correct” cash weight, only what risk each one carries. 30% here is a drawdown budget, labelled as the preference it is. Correction: earlier revisions claimed the illustration below was itself invariant. Pinning IEMG changes the risky mix, not just its scale, so it drifts from 0.114 to 0.108.
Variance math breaks on BMNR
24.5%
Under a lognormal model, a +10% compound return at 109% volatility implies an arithmetic mean of 69.6%. Nobody forecasts that, so the two assumptions are jointly incoherent and every variance-based statistic involving BMNR is approximate. A rebalancing-premium adjustment was computed, found to be dominated by this artefact, and discarded. It is a further argument for the 5% cap.

Is this the best available?

Every allocation the brief permits — all four sleeves, multiples of 5, none below 5% — is 969 portfolios. Plotting each by net CAGR against normalised drawdown gives the efficient frontier: the set where no other allocation offers both more return and less risk.

The recommended book is efficient; the baseline, by a hair, is not. Searched exhaustively, nothing beats QQQ 35 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 on both axes at once. The baseline is beaten by exactly one allocation — QQQ 15 / IEMG 60 / SGOV 20 / BMNR 5 — and by +0.01 points of CAGR and 0.1 of drawdown, which is a technicality rather than a verdict. The movement is the point: through Rev. 18 the baseline sat on the frontier and the EM tilt had lost its last justification; repricing both funds to their current multiples has put the two equity sleeves level on risk-adjusted return and pulled the curve back toward emerging markets. The gap the recommendation costs is now 0.32 points of CAGR.
Three honest limits. “Best” is a curve, not a point — which allocation is right depends on the drawdown you will actually tolerate, and both books are defensible answers — 107 allocations are efficient. The curve is close to straight rather than bending: at the recommendation one more point of drawdown buys about 0.09 points of CAGR, and that ratio barely changes along its length — which is the frontier telling you what the capital-allocation line above already did. And beyond BMNR 45% the frontier returns drawdowns worse than −100%, which is impossible — the variance model fails exactly where this page already documents it failing, so the curve is drawn only where BMNR stays at 5%.

Bands are annualised percent, on the ten-year realised CAGR. The standard error is the book’s own volatility divided by √10 — the sampling error of a ten-year average, before any error in the inputs themselves.

No allocation on this page is provable inside its own horizon. Of the 969 admissible allocations, the number statistically distinguishable from the recommendation at 95% over ten years is 0. The reason is arithmetic, not modesty: for two books drawn from the same four sleeves, z ≈ IR × √T, and an information ratio of 0.20 gives z = 0.62 where 1.96 is needed. Establishing that the baseline really out-returns the recommendation would take 99 years; establishing that the recommended book beats plain T-bills would take 63. The most separable alternative in the whole set — QQQ 40 / IEMG 35 / SGOV 20 / BMNR 5 — would still need 43.

Why the ranking is firmer than the number

The level is close to unknowable
±4.81 pts
One standard error on the ten-year outcome is 4.81 points — roughly two thirds of the forecast itself. Anyone quoting 7.54% to two decimals, this page included, is reporting the centre of a distribution running from about −2% to +17% at 95%. The six-figure terminal values elsewhere on this page carry the same error; they are shown to the dollar because the arithmetic is exact, not because the outcome is.
The comparison is nine times sharper
±0.52 pts
The two books share three of four sleeves, so their difference is a 10-point switch out of QQQ with a tracking volatility of just 1.65%/yr — a standard error of 0.52 points, not 4.81. Errors common to both books cancel. The 0.32-point gap is therefore 0.62 SE, which makes it a 73% chance the baseline ends ahead on return — worth acting on, even though it is nowhere near provable.
A correction to this page’s own arithmetic. An earlier pass put that figure at 53%, by dividing the gap by the ±4.81-point error on the level. Wrong denominator: a comparison of overlapping books is governed by the tracking error of the difference, not by either book’s absolute error. Against the right one the answer is 73%. The page had understated its own confidence in the ranking while overstating what it knew about the number.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Over ten years cash drag compounds into a real cost — SGOV’s $14,506 terminal value against QQQ’s $24,805 on the same $10,000. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not how much equity to own.
The return model has swung three times. The rankings favour Asia and the valuation gap favours EM. Correcting the terminal-multiple asymmetry put QQQ ahead on raw return; correcting its stale starting multiple put it ahead on risk-adjusted return too; and tying both multiples to price in Rev. 19 has put the two level again, 0.272 against 0.274. The EM weight was trimmed 40% → 35% → 30% along the way and has been held there rather than chasing each swing back.
The tilt is a risk trade that has become nearly free. IEMG forecasts 8.73% net against QQQ’s 9.51% — it still earns less — but per unit of volatility the two are level, and it buys a shallower normalised drawdown at half the fee. The claim that it improves both sides at once remains withdrawn; the claim that it costs risk-adjusted return, made while QQQ led on that measure from Rev. 10 to Rev. 18, is withdrawn too.
Volatility sizes the book, it does not steer it. Re-running with volatility mean-reverted to 18.9 and correlations stressed to 0.85 leaves the Sharpe ordering of every candidate intact — so the tilt is robust to the regime. The risk estimate is not: −25.9% at today’s levels becomes −33.6% normalised. Sizing to the second is why the reserve is 30%.
Where the model was overruled. Subject only to the brief’s 5%-per-sleeve floor, the best return-per-unit-risk mix is QQQ 40 / IEMG 50 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — Sharpe 0.260 against the recommended 0.246. Rejected: it over-fits the two least reliable inputs and holds only 5% in reserve, which is not a drawdown budget anyone would choose. A rebalancing premium of an apparent +2.9%/yr was also computed and discarded as an artefact of BMNR’s 109% volatility.
SGOV at 30% is a preference, not an output. Scaling a fixed risky mix against cash holds return-per-drawdown at 0.1117 whatever the cash weight — the optimizer genuinely cannot pick it. 30% is a stated drawdown budget of roughly −34% in a normalised world. Held in 0–3 month bills, the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields and earns 3.79% while waiting.
BMNR is capped at 5% for risk, not weight. At a twentieth of capital it contributes 24.5% of variance — a 4.9× risk-to-weight ratio — and at 0.65 correlation to QQQ it fails in the same regimes. Its forecast has been cut twice in a fortnight — the filed 2.62% staking yield replacing an assumed 3.00%, then a premium widening to 1.08× on crypto as the stock outran its treasury — to 9.05%. 5% remains the largest defensible size: total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.117% as a by-product of the rotation — $24 per $10,000 over ten years, taken with no risk added.
Recommendation. Adopt QQQ 35 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 if you want the lower drawdown — −33.6% normalised against the baseline’s −36.1%, bought with 0.32 points of CAGR. If you do not, the baseline is a defensible book too — it returns 0.32 points more for 2.5 points more drawdown, at essentially the same return per unit of risk. Rebalance semi-annually or on a 5-point drift. Two triggers would change the answer. The first is the thesis: revisit if the EM forward-multiple discount to developed markets narrows from about 40% toward its 25% norm, since its closing removes the overweight entirely. The second is volatility: a sustained VIX above 18.9 makes 30% cash a candidate to spend toward 25%. That trigger is now 3.55 points away; on decision day it stood 1.19 away and this page said the premise was nearly spent. At 15.35 the two regimes differ by 7.7 points of drawdown on the recommended book, and the reserve is sized to the wider of the two.
