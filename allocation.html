Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

IEMG
Tailwind

A committee unanimous on tightening means renewed dollar strength, and Brent at $103.21 is a terms-of-trade hit to a bloc that imports most of its energy. The overweight rests on a ten-year valuation gap, not the next FOMC.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and a 0.65 correlation to QQQ means it fails when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification — though the company has pivoted from accumulating ETH to buying back stock.
The core tension. Growth is genuine, but it is met by tightening policy, a rising long end, a realised energy shock and still-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full ten-year horizon while tilting the equity mix toward the cheaper bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than avoid it.

Where this understates risk. Square-root scaling assumes constant volatility and normal returns. Both fail in stress: volatility clusters and equity tails are fatter than lognormal. Two findings that constrained the answer

The cash line is straight
ret/DD 0.1117
Hold the risky mix in fixed proportion and scale it against cash, and return-per-drawdown is 0.1117 at every point — invariant to machine precision, because an uncorrelated zero-variance sleeve scales return and risk by the same factor. That is a theorem: no volatility analysis can identify a “correct” cash weight, only what risk each one carries. 30% here is a drawdown budget, labelled as the preference it is. Correction: earlier revisions claimed the illustration below was itself invariant. Pinning IEMG changes the risky mix, not just its scale, so it drifts from 0.115 to 0.108.
Variance math breaks on BMNR
69.6%
Under a lognormal model, a +10% compound return at 109% volatility implies an arithmetic mean of 69.6%. Nobody forecasts that, so the two assumptions are jointly incoherent and every variance-based statistic involving BMNR is approximate. A rebalancing-premium adjustment was computed, found to be dominated by this artefact, and discarded. It is a further argument for the 5% cap.

Is this the best available?

Every allocation the brief permits — all four sleeves, multiples of 5, none below 5% — is 969 portfolios. Plotting each by net CAGR against normalised drawdown gives the efficient frontier: the set where no other allocation offers both more return and less risk.

Both books now sit on the frontier. Searched exhaustively, nothing beats either on both axes at once — so both are efficient, and they differ only in where they sit on the curve. That is a change: last pass the baseline was dominated by three allocations. Correcting QQQ’s stale multiple lifted it onto the frontier. The optimized book is therefore no longer an improvement on the baseline; it is a lower-drawdown point on the same curve, bought with 0.38 points of CAGR.
Three honest limits. “Best” is a curve, not a point — which allocation is right depends on the drawdown you will actually tolerate, and both books are defensible answers — 90 allocations are efficient. The curve is close to straight rather than bending: at the recommendation one more point of drawdown buys about 0.14 points of CAGR, and that ratio barely changes along its length — which is the frontier telling you what the capital-allocation line above already did. And beyond BMNR 45% the frontier returns drawdowns worse than −100%, which is impossible — the variance model fails exactly where this page already documents it failing, so the curve is drawn only where BMNR stays at 5%.

Bands are annualised percent, on the ten-year realised CAGR. The standard error is the book’s own volatility divided by √10 — the sampling error of a ten-year average, before any error in the inputs themselves.

No allocation on this page is provable inside its own horizon. Of the 969 admissible allocations, the number statistically distinguishable from the recommendation at 95% over ten years is 0. The reason is arithmetic, not modesty: for two books drawn from the same four sleeves, z ≈ IR × √T, and an information ratio of 0.23 gives z = 0.73 where 1.96 is needed. Establishing that the baseline really out-returns the recommendation would take 72 years; establishing that the recommended book beats plain T-bills would take 60. The most separable alternative in the whole set — QQQ 50 / IEMG 40 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — would still need 41.

Why the ranking is firmer than the number

The level is close to unknowable
±4.81 pts
One standard error on the ten-year outcome is 4.81 points — roughly two thirds of the forecast itself. Anyone quoting 7.64% to two decimals, this page included, is reporting the centre of a distribution running from about −2% to +17% at 95%. The six-figure terminal values elsewhere on this page carry the same error; they are shown to the dollar because the arithmetic is exact, not because the outcome is.
The comparison is nine times sharper
±0.52 pts
The two books share three of four sleeves, so their difference is a 10-point switch out of QQQ with a tracking volatility of just 1.65%/yr — a standard error of 0.52 points, not 4.81. Errors common to both books cancel. The 0.38-point gap is therefore 0.73 SE, which makes it a 77% chance the baseline ends ahead on return — worth acting on, even though it is nowhere near provable.
A correction to this page’s own arithmetic. An earlier pass put that figure at 53%, by dividing the gap by the ±4.81-point error on the level. Wrong denominator: a comparison of overlapping books is governed by the tracking error of the difference, not by either book’s absolute error. Against the right one the answer is 77%. The page had understated its own confidence in the ranking while overstating what it knew about the number.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Over ten years cash drag compounds into a real cost — SGOV’s $14,506 terminal value against QQQ’s $25,843 on the same $10,000. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not how much equity to own.
All three signals have now turned. The rankings still favour Asia and the 11.7×-against-22.4× gap still favours EM — but the return model has reversed twice. Correcting the terminal-multiple asymmetry put QQQ ahead on raw return; correcting its stale starting multiple put it ahead on risk-adjusted return too, and it stays ahead at 0.29 against 0.26. The EM position has been trimmed 40% → 35% → 30% and is now only five points above the baseline’s 25%.
The tilt is a risk trade, not a free lunch. IEMG forecasts 8.49% net against QQQ’s 9.96% — it earns less — and per unit of volatility it trails too, 0.26 against 0.29. It buys a barely shallower drawdown (−39% against −40%) at half the fee, and nothing else. The claims that it improved both sides at once, and then that it kept a risk-adjusted edge, are both withdrawn.
Volatility sizes the book, it does not steer it. Re-running with volatility mean-reverted to 18.9 and correlations stressed to 0.85 leaves the Sharpe ordering of every candidate intact — so the tilt is robust to the regime. The risk estimate is not: −25.9% at today’s levels becomes −34.5% normalised. Sizing to the second is why the reserve is 30%.
Where the model was overruled. Subject only to the brief’s 5%-per-sleeve floor, the best return-per-unit-risk mix is QQQ 60 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — Sharpe 0.268 against the recommended 0.253. Rejected: it over-fits the two least reliable inputs and holds only 5% in reserve, which is not a drawdown budget anyone would choose. A rebalancing premium of an apparent +2.9%/yr was also computed and discarded as an artefact of BMNR’s 109% volatility.
SGOV at 30% is a preference, not an output. Scaling a fixed risky mix against cash holds return-per-drawdown at 0.1117 whatever the cash weight — the optimizer genuinely cannot pick it. 30% is a stated drawdown budget of roughly −34% in a normalised world. Held in 0–3 month bills, the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields and earns 3.79% while waiting.
BMNR is capped at 5% for risk, not weight. At a twentieth of capital it contributes 23.8% of variance — a 4.8× risk-to-weight ratio — and at 0.65 correlation to QQQ it fails in the same regimes. Verification cut its forecast this pass (2.61% staking, not 3.00%) while improving its balance sheet (0.98× total NAV, $4B of buybacks replacing dilution), but 5% remains the largest defensible size: total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.117% as a by-product of the rotation — $26 per $10,000 over ten years, taken with no risk added.
Recommendation. Adopt QQQ 35 / IEMG 30 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 if you want the lower drawdown — −34.5% normalised against the baseline’s −37.1%, bought with 0.38 points of CAGR. If you do not, the baseline is now the better book on every other measure, and this page says so rather than defending a tilt the evidence has stopped supporting. Rebalance semi-annually or on a 5-point drift. Revisit if the EM forward-multiple discount narrows from 40% toward its 25% norm — that is the thesis, and its closing removes the overweight entirely. That second trigger has moved back out to 4.09 points. The page said to revisit if the VIX sustained a move above 18.9; a week ago it stood 1.19 points away and this page said the premise was nearly spent. At 14.81 the two regimes differ by 9.2 points of drawdown again, and the case for holding 30% in reserve is as strong as it was at the start of the month. The trigger stands: one sustained session above 18.9 and 30% cash becomes a candidate to spend toward 25%.

Both dividend yields were priced against stale share prices. QQQ’s income term carried 0.65% and IEMG’s 2.26%. QQQ’s forecast falls 0.23 points and IEMG’s 0.10, which narrows QQQ’s risk-adjusted lead from four hundredths to two without reversing it.

Two unsourced assumptions, replaced with data. The QQQ·IEMG correlation had been set at 0.72 by judgement; the pair’s actual figure is 0.66 all-time (0.82 over one year, which supports the 0.85 stressed case). And IEMG’s maximum drawdown was assumed at −33% when its realised worst since inception is −38.71%. Over the window both funds have existed, IEMG drew down more than QQQ, not seven points less — the same asymmetry as the terminal multiples, favouring the same sleeve. Corrected to −39%, IEMG’s “shallower drawdown” advantage over QQQ shrinks from seven points to one.

The stale multiple, resolved. QQQ’s 25.2× forward P/E was flagged as the weakest input for three passes and is now corrected to 22.4×. Three independent routes agree: two secondary sources put the Nasdaq-100 at 20.8–22.4× in late August, and the trailing multiple fell 19% over the same window (35.24× → 28.55×), which drags the forward figure to about 20.4× on its own. None supports 25.2× as current. Continuing to hold it because its source was better-pedigreed would have been preferring provenance to accuracy. The correction adds 1.16 points to QQQ and, with it, reverses the last remaining argument for the emerging-markets tilt: QQQ now leads on risk-adjusted return as well as raw, and the baseline has moved onto the efficient frontier. The recommendation is kept as the lower-drawdown point on that frontier, and labelled as a risk preference rather than an improvement.

The two that mattered most before that. The terminal multiples were asymmetric: IEMG’s was anchored to its own 10-year average, QQQ’s hand-set at 21× — below its own average of 22.9×. One rule for both adds 0.85 points to QQQ and reverses the ranking, so the claim that IEMG improved return and risk together is withdrawn. And the VIX was described as both “near its 10-year average” and 24% below it; the 2016–2023 mean is 18.9.

Still open. This list previously carried the stale 25.2× multiple as an open question after the same log recorded it resolved to 22.4× — a contradiction inside one card, now removed. What is genuinely open is narrower and worse: three inputs remain assumptions rather than observations. BMNR’s 9%/yr ETH appreciation is the largest unsourced number on the page, and the sleeve is capped at 5% precisely because of it. The 1.70 × σ multiplier for ten-year expected drawdown is a rule of thumb, which is why it is reported as the 1.65–1.75 band. And QQQ’s 9.5%/yr earnings growth extrapolates a cycle across a decade at a rate no decade has sustained.
