Correlated transmission

The same six drivers do not hit the four sleeves the same way. This is where the composite becomes an allocation.

SGOV
Tailwind
A hiking Fed is the one regime where a 0–3 month bill sleeve is paid to wait, and at 90% odds that hike is close to locked in. Its 3.63% SEC yield against the 3.4% headline CPI confirmed on 11 September leaves a real return of about +0.2% at near-zero duration — thin, and the September CPI carrying the Saudi shock is the print that could take it negative — and every hawkish surprise that hurts the other three sleeves raises its reinvestment rate.
QQQ
Two-sided
The strongest single beneficiary of the capex and productivity boom the Fed is describing — and the sleeve most exposed to rates, with the 10-year at 4.78% and the 2-year at ~4.36%, its highest since January 2025. Long-duration equity discounts back at a rising rate. Earnings support is real; the multiple is the risk.
IEMG
Tailwind
Captures the fastest-growing bloc — the IMF has China at 4.4% and India at 6.3% for 2026, together 43.6% of all global growth — at 11.7× forward earnings against the Nasdaq-100’s 25.2×. The near-term premise has flipped. Earlier drafts rested this sleeve partly on a Fed that held rather than hiked, capping dollar strength. With a September hike now the base case, renewed dollar strength is the live headwind, and the −1.50%/yr currency drag in the forecast is the assumption carrying the thesis. And a second headwind has now stacked on the first: Brent near $100 is a terms-of-trade hit to a bloc that imports most of its energy. The overweight survives on a ten-year valuation gap, not on the next FOMC meeting — but that trigger has now fired: a third consecutive pass weakened the case, this time on the return model rather than the macro, and the tilt was trimmed from 40% to 35%.
BMNR
Headwind
A levered ETH proxy at the wrong end of every driver: tightening policy drains the liquidity crypto beta feeds on, and a 0.65 correlation to QQQ means it fails precisely when the equity sleeve fails. Convexity, not diversification — though the company has now pivoted from accumulating ETH to buying back its own stock, which changes the dilution picture materially.
The core tension. Growth is genuine and broad, but it is being met by tightening policy, a rising long end and record-cheap volatility. That argues for staying invested at a full 10-year horizon while shifting the equity mix toward the cheaper, faster-growing bloc — and holding a real cash reserve to buy the repricing rather than to avoid it. Re-scoring every driver against primary sources moved two of the six, and left the composite unchanged at 2.7 on the 1–5 scale.

Term structure math

Where this understates risk. Square-root scaling assumes constant volatility and normal returns. Both fail in stress: volatility clusters, and equity tails are fatter than lognormal. At VIX 15.30 the market is pricing a 12-month band of ±25.2% against a 2016–2023 average of 18.9 — implied volatility is still roughly 19% cheap to its own history, heading into a 15–16 September FOMC now 90% priced for a hike, an August CPI that came in hot on core, and an active conflict around the Strait of Hormuz.

Two findings that constrained the answer

The cash line is straight
ret/DD 0.1208
Hold the risky mix in fixed proportion and scale it against cash, and return-per-drawdown is 0.1208 at every point — invariant to machine precision, because an uncorrelated zero-variance sleeve scales return and risk by the same factor. That is a theorem, not a result: no amount of volatility analysis can identify a “correct” cash weight. It can only say what risk each one carries. The 30% here is a drawdown budget, labelled as the preference it is. Correction: earlier revisions claimed the illustration below was itself invariant. It is not — pinning IEMG at 40 while trading QQQ against SGOV changes the risky mix, not just its scale, so the ratio drifts from 0.123 to 0.118 across the range. The theorem holds; the illustration only approximates it.
Variance math breaks on BMNR
69.6%
Under a lognormal model, a +10% compound return at 109% volatility implies an arithmetic mean of 69.6%. Nobody forecasts that. The two assumptions are jointly incoherent, which means every variance-based statistic involving BMNR is approximate at best. A rebalancing-premium adjustment was computed, found to be dominated by this artefact, and discarded rather than published. It is a further argument for the 5% cap: keep the position small enough that model breakdown there cannot corrupt the portfolio conclusion.

Ten-year regime view

Over a decade a portfolio of this composition should expect to live through two to three volatility regime shifts. QQQ's own history sets the honest bound on what the equity sleeve can do:

2000–02 dot-com≈−83%
2007–09 financial crisis−50 to −53%
2020 COVID crash≈−27%
2022 rate-hiking cycle−33 to −37%

Published figures vary by a few points depending on price versus total-return basis and intraday versus closing marks, so these are ranges rather than points. The 2022 episode is the closest analogue to the present configuration — a rate-driven de-rating rather than a credit event — and the −40% QQQ assumption deliberately sits beyond its worst reading, since the starting multiple today is higher. The dot-com figure is the reminder that concentration risk in this index has no theoretical floor, and the reason the equity core is split across two blocs rather than held in one.

Rationale

Start from the horizon, not the headlines. Ten years is long enough that cash drag compounds into a serious cost — SGOV’s $13,649 terminal value against QQQ’s $23,739 on the same $10,000 is the price of caution. The macro signal is therefore spent on which equities to own, not on how much equity to own. Total risk assets stay at 75% in both portfolios.
The equity rotation follows three independent signals. The regional rankings put Asia/EM ahead of the US at every horizon. The valuation data puts EM at 11.7× forward against the Nasdaq-100’s 25.2×, a 40% discount to developed markets versus a 25% long-run norm. The third signal has now reversed. Correcting the terminal-multiple asymmetry puts QQQ 0.44 points ahead of IEMG on raw return, where earlier revisions had IEMG ahead by 0.76. Two of the three signals still favour EM — the rankings and the valuation gap — but the return model no longer does, and the tilt was trimmed from 40% to 35% accordingly.
Volatility analysis sizes the book, it does not steer it. The tilt is unchanged when the model is re-run with volatility mean-reverted to its 18.9 average and correlations stressed to 0.85 — the Sharpe ordering of every candidate allocation survives, so the EM overweight is robust to the volatility regime. What does not survive is the risk estimate. At today’s levels the book looks like −25.8%; normalised, it is −33.3%. Sizing to the second number rather than the first is the entire reason the reserve rose from 25% to 30%.
The tilt is now a risk trade, not a free lunch. IEMG forecasts 8.59% net against QQQ’s 9.03% — it earns less — but on 18% volatility against 21%, a −33% drawdown against −40%, and half the fee. Per unit of volatility it still leads, 0.30 against 0.28. Earlier revisions claimed this sleeve improved both sides at once; that claim is withdrawn. The optimized book now buys 2.9 points of drawdown with 0.34 points of CAGR, and says so.
Where the model was overruled — twice. Searching every 5%-increment allocation, subject only to the brief’s own floor of 5% per sleeve, the highest return-per-unit-risk mix is QQQ 25 / IEMG 65 / SGOV 5 / BMNR 5 — Sharpe 0.278 against the recommended book’s 0.263. It moved toward QQQ this pass as that sleeve’s forecast was corrected upward. That answer is rejected: it over-fits a point estimate whose two least reliable inputs — the valuation reversion and the currency drag — are precisely what drive it, and it puts 65% of a dollar investor’s capital in a bloc where China alone is roughly a quarter of the index, while holding only 5% in reserve. Capping the tilt at 40% keeps most of the benefit while leaving the conclusion robust to those assumptions being wrong. Separately, a rebalancing-premium adjustment worth an apparent +2.9%/yr was computed and then discarded once it proved to be an artefact of BMNR’s 109% volatility rather than a real return.
SGOV rises to 30% as ammunition, not as fear — and the number is a preference, not an output. Scaling a fixed risky mix against cash holds return-per-drawdown at 0.1208 regardless of how much cash you hold — invariant to machine precision. The optimizer genuinely cannot pick this weight; 30% is a stated drawdown budget of roughly −33% in a normalised-volatility world. Holding it in 0–3 month bills rather than long bonds means the reserve is not itself damaged by rising yields, and it earns 3.63% while waiting to be sold into the drawdown.
BMNR is capped at 5% because of risk, not weight. At a twentieth of the capital it contributes 24.7% of portfolio variance — a 4.9× risk-to-weight ratio. It is also a poor diversifier: 0.65 correlated to QQQ, it fails in the same regimes as the growth sleeve. Verification did improve its standing — the premium is 1.02× on total NAV of $15.6B, not the 1.10× that crypto holdings alone imply — but with severe dilution history and an 80.5% drawdown already on the tape, 5% remains the largest defensible position. It earns its place as convexity to an ETH recovery, sized so total loss costs 5% of capital.
Fees are a controlled variable. Weighted expense falls from 0.126% to 0.108% purely as a by-product of the rotation, since IEMG and SGOV both charge 0.09% against QQQ’s 0.18%. Over ten years that is $36 saved per $10,000 — small, but return taken with no risk added. QQQ’s own cut from 0.20% is worth about $41 per $10,000 over the same period.
Recommendation. Adopt the optimized allocation — QQQ 30 / IEMG 35 / SGOV 30 / BMNR 5 — and rebalance to target semi-annually or whenever any sleeve drifts more than 5 points. Note the margin is now thin: return-per-risk is 0.263 against the baseline’s 0.262, so the case rests on the drawdown difference rather than on risk-adjusted superiority. Rebalancing is doing most of the work here: it mechanically trims the sleeve that has run and buys the one that has fallen, which is what turns BMNR’s 95% volatility from a liability into a small, systematic source of return. One of the stated revisit triggers has already fired. The condition was “if the Fed hikes rather than holds”; after the payrolls beat, the Saudi supply shock and a hot core CPI, a September hike is priced at 90%. Two forward-looking claims resolved this pass. The August CPI landed on 11 September at 3.4% headline and 2.4% core — but core rose 0.3% month-on-month against 0.2% expected, and CME FedWatch odds of a September hike jumped from ~70% to 90%. The page had been carrying ~59%, so that figure has swung between 55% and 90% inside a fortnight. Separately, the ECB hiked to 2.50% unanimously on 10 September; this page had recorded the economists’ view that it would be the terminal move, and that has already been overtaken — investors are betting on more, and Lagarde said the Council did not discuss the path. One caveat worth keeping in view: the August CPI window closes before the 8 September Saudi strikes, so the sharpest part of the energy shock is not in this data at all.

On the terminal multiples — the largest error found so far. The two equity sleeves were not treated the same way. IEMG’s terminal multiple was anchored to its own 10-year average of 12.2×, which is defensible. QQQ’s was set by hand at 21× — below its own 10-year average of 22.9×. One sleeve was held to its history and the other marked down past it, and the asymmetry ran in favour of the emerging-markets tilt, which is this page’s central recommendation. Applying one rule to both adds 0.85 points to QQQ and reverses the ranking: QQQ now out-earns IEMG, 9.03% against 8.59%. The claim that IEMG improves return and risk simultaneously is withdrawn. The tilt survives on risk-adjusted grounds — 0.30 return per unit of volatility against 0.28 — and was trimmed from 40% to 35%, honouring the trigger this page set two passes ago.

A caveat on the starting multiple. The 25.2× used for QQQ is Siblis Research as of 1 July and is now two months stale. Several secondary sources put the Nasdaq-100 at 20.8–22.4× forward in late August, consistent with the index having sold off since June. Those sources are not first-tier, so the authoritative figure is retained — but note the direction: if they are right, QQQ’s forecast rises by roughly another 1.2 points and the case for the EM tilt weakens further rather than recovering.

On the oil shock. This pass caught a genuine regime change rather than a data slip. Iran-aligned strikes halted Saudi output, Brent reached $99.16, and September hike odds rose to 68%. Four of the six drivers moved down and the composite fell from 2.7 to 2.5 — its first real move in four passes. It also cuts against the emerging-markets tilt from a new direction: Asia imports most of its energy, so this is a terms-of-trade hit on top of the dollar headwind. The 3-, 6- and 12-month Asia scores were all cut; only the 10-year score stands.

On the cash-line claim. The page asserted that trading QQQ against SGOV gave an identical return-per-drawdown ratio at every step. That was overstated. Pinning IEMG at 40 changes the risky mix, not merely its scale, so the ratio drifts from 0.115 to 0.114. Scaling a genuinely fixed risky mix against cash is invariant — to 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁷ — so the conclusion survives, but the demonstration behind it did not, and has been replaced with one that does.

On the September hike. The 4 September payrolls print — 162,000 against a 53,000 consensus — moved futures to roughly a 58–62% chance of a hike on 15–16 September. That retired a premise this page had been resting on: that a Fed on hold was capping dollar strength for the emerging-markets sleeve. The overweight now stands on the ten-year valuation gap alone, with the −1.50%/yr currency drag as the assumption carrying it, and the Asia 3-month score was cut to reflect the near-term headwind.

On the EM terminal multiple. The forecast assumed emerging markets re-rate from 11.6× to 12.5× over the decade. Fresh data shows 11.7× forward sits below the 10-year average of 12.2× but level with the 20-year average of 11.7× — so 12.5× was above both anchors. Re-anchoring to 12.2× takes 0.32 points off IEMG’s forecast and narrows its edge over QQQ from 0.76 to 0.44 points. The tilt survives, but on a thinner margin than previously shown.

On the frontier claim. The stated unconstrained optimum was previously QQQ 5 / IEMG 70 / SGOV 20 — an artefact of a 20% cash floor left over from an earlier run and never stated as an assumption. Searched against the brief’s actual floor of 5% per sleeve, the optimizer wants 85% in emerging markets. The rejection stands, and reads more strongly for being honest about what was rejected.

On the gauge rescale. Mapping 1–10 to 1–5 is 1+(x−1)×4⁄9, not division by two, since both scales floor at 1. Doing it properly exposed a bug in the original arc: it filled value/10 = 47.8% when the scale’s floor is 1, so it should have read (4.78−1)/9 = 42.0%. The new arc reads 41.9% — the near-identical figure a pure rescale must produce, which is what confirms both the fix and the conversion. The regional rankings were then put through the same map, so both instruments now read on one scale; their horizon bars fill on (score−1)/4 too, and the point gaps between blocs shrink by the 4⁄9 factor without any ordering changing.

The two that mattered most. The page previously stated both that the VIX “sits near its 10-year average” and that the average was near 19 — a direct self-contradiction; the 2016–2023 mean is 18.9, so spot at 14.32 is ~24% below it. And QQQ’s trailing returns were carried from a 30 June factsheet without being marked stale, overstating the run-up by 8 points and making the US sleeve look more extended than it is.
